Phone / window example

The order starts
with a conversation.

  1. Customer finds a menu post or arrives at the window.
  2. Staff explains sizes, options, and pricing.
  3. Staff takes the order and enters or writes it down.
  4. Kitchen receives the details and prepares the food.
  5. Customer pays and collects the order.

If ordering happens on arrival, the conversation and preparation can occupy the pickup line. Phone orders still need staff attention.

Proposed online drive-through pickup

The order is settled
before the window.

  1. Browse. Search the real menu and see size prices.
  2. Build. Choose a sandwich, approved modifiers, fries, and a drink.
  3. Schedule. Pick an available time and choose the drive-through window.
  4. Confirm. Receive an order reference; kitchen gets a marked ticket.
  5. Collect. After the ready confirmation, arrive for pickup and give the reference at the window.

A shorter handoff is the goal. Prep times, order limits, staffing, and an overflow plan determine whether the line actually improves.

    See every formula and the model’s limits

    Let B = existing daily pickup orders, D = open days/month, A = current average order, S = online share as a decimal, N = genuinely new daily orders, L = extra add-on dollars/order, C = variable cost share, p₀/f₀ = current POS percentage/fixed fee, p₁/f₁ = online percentage/fixed fee, M = monthly service, K = setup cost, H = setup allocation months.

    Existing online orders
    Q = B × D × S
    New online orders
    R = N × D
    Additional revenue
    R × A + (Q + R) × L
    Variable costs
    Additional revenue × C
    Processing change
    R × ((A + L) × p₁ + f₁) + Q × (((A + L) × p₁ + f₁) − (A × p₀ + f₀))
    Monthly contribution
    Additional revenue − variable costs − processing change − M
    After setup allocation
    Monthly contribution − K ÷ H
    Simple setup payback
    K ÷ monthly contribution, if contribution > 0. If K = 0, no setup cost to recover.
    Attention freed (hours)
    Q × minutes avoided per migrated order ÷ 60
    New daily orders to break even
    Max(0, (M + K ÷ H − Q × L × (1 − C) + Q × (((A + L) × p₁ + f₁) − (A × p₀ + f₀))) ÷ (D × ((A + L) × (1 − C − p₁) − f₁))). If each added order contributes zero or less, more orders cannot resolve a deficit in this model.

    All new orders are assumed to be online. The same add-on lift and variable cost share apply to new and migrated orders. The model does not double-count existing base sales. Processing savings are possible if the online effective rate is lower; no commission savings are assumed. Fractional orders represent a daily average.

    Sales tax/VAT, tips, discounts, refunds, chargebacks, marketing, additional fixed labor, rent, utilities, equipment, and financing are excluded unless included in the entered costs. Current average order and fee calculations should exclude sales tax and tips for a like-for-like comparison. This is additional contribution, not accounting net profit. It assumes sufficient demand and kitchen/window capacity and does not establish that new demand will occur.

    Setup allocation spreads the one-time cost for comparison; it is not a cash payment schedule. Payback assumes the monthly outcome repeats and ignores time value of money. Hero House’s economics are unknown. Values are saved only in this browser; reset with a scenario button.

    A measured first launch

    Prove it at
    the pickup window.

    A small drive-through pilot can show whether the website improves the handoff and earns more than it costs. Choose a period with enough ordinary and busy days to compare.

    Agree before launch

    Confirm the menu, prices, modifiers, window instructions, fees, and kitchen connection. Set prep times, pickup caps, payment/refund rules, and a pause/overflow procedure.

    Measure during the pilot

    Track completed online orders, average ticket, side/drink attachment, truly new customers or occasions, fees, food/packaging costs, cancellations, and late orders. Time the window handoff and watch the line.

    Decide from the margin

    Compare similar days and the owner’s actual costs. Retain the channel if additional contribution and service benefits justify the cost. Adjust slots, menu suggestions, or staffing where results show friction.
